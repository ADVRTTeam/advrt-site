Training has an economic quirk that should change how its marketing is run, and usually does not.

Once a course is scheduled, the trainer is booked and the room is paid for. Whether twelve people turn up or five, most of the cost is already committed. So the difference between a half-full course and a full one is very close to pure margin, and the marginal seat is the most profitable thing you will sell that month.

That has a direct consequence: your campaigns should be pointed at specific dates with empty seats, not at courses as abstract products.

## Date-led, not course-led

Most training accounts are built as a permanent brochure. One campaign per course, running at a steady budget, forever.

It works, in the sense that enquiries arrive. But it ignores the only variable that matters, which is which dates actually need filling. You end up spending the same to promote a course that sold out three weeks ago as one running on Thursday with four seats left.

A date-led account inverts that. Spend follows the gaps:

- **Courses with empty seats and a near date get pushed hard.** Urgency is real and worth paying for.
- **Courses that are nearly full ease off.** Why pay to create a waiting list?
- **Courses that are full come off entirely**, until the next date opens.

This takes a feed of your fill rates and a willingness to move budget weekly rather than quarterly. In return, you stop paying to advertise things you cannot sell.

> Advertising a sold-out course is the most expensive way to build a waiting list.

## Two buyers, two journeys

The second structural issue: training has two completely different customers arriving through similar searches.

**The individual** is making a career decision. A labourer needing a CSCS card, an electrician going for 18th Edition, someone retraining into retrofit assessment. The purchase is personal, often self-funded, and the message that works is about what the qualification opens up. Mobile-first, booking in a few taps, prices and dates visible without a form.

**The employer** is solving an operational problem. A site needs five people with SMSTS by next month, or a tender requires certain accreditations. The buyer is an HR manager or a contracts manager, the decision is budgeted, and the sale is larger. What works here is group pricing, on-site delivery options, invoicing, and account management for repeat bookings.

Same course, same search terms sometimes, entirely different pages and entirely different follow-up. Running one journey for both means underserving the more valuable one.

## Put the practical details on the page

Training buyers want the same four facts, and withholding any of them costs you the booking: what dates, where, how much, and can I get it funded.

Hiding prices behind an enquiry form is particularly costly here, because the purchase is small enough and the alternatives numerous enough that nobody will fill in a form to find out. They will just check the next provider.

Dates and locations are the same. "Courses run regularly, contact us for dates" means the visitor leaves. A live list of dates with availability is the single highest-value thing on a training page.

## Funding is a feature

Providers often treat funding as a complication to be explained on a phone call. Treated properly it is one of the strongest hooks you have, particularly in retrofit and site-skills areas where funded routes are a genuine draw.

The approach that works: say plainly on the page which routes exist and roughly who qualifies, then capture eligibility on the booking form. Employment status, location, whether an employer is paying, any scheme they have been referred through.

That way the enquiry arrives with its funding position already known, and the call is about confirming a place rather than unpicking eligibility from scratch. Faster for you, clearer for them, and it stops you spending call time on people who were never eligible for the route that brought them in.

## Then keep them

A last point that gets overlooked: most training is recurring. Certificates expire, refreshers come due, cards need renewing.

A learner who had a good experience is the cheapest booking you will ever get, and an employer who booked five people once will book five more. Capturing expiry dates at booking and running a simple reminder campaign against them is low effort and compounds every year, which is more than can be said for most of the advertising budget.
