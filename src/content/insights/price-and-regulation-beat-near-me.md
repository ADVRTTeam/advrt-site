When a specialist trade firm decides to take search seriously, the first instinct is almost always the same: target the service plus the town. Asbestos survey Birmingham. Fire risk assessment Glasgow. EICR Cardiff.

It is an understandable instinct and it is backwards.

## What the data shows

Across the compliance disciplines we run lead generation brands in, we can group non-branded queries by what the searcher is actually trying to do. Three groups cover most of it.

**Price and cost.** What does this cost, how much should I expect to pay, is this quote reasonable.

**Obligation and regulation.** Is this a legal requirement, how often must it be done, who is responsible, what happens if I do not.

**Supplier selection.** Who does this near me, best providers, companies in my area.

In every discipline we have looked at, the first two groups together substantially outnumber the third — by roughly three to one. And they consistently rank considerably worse. Supplier-selection queries are where everyone competes, so positions there are respectable. The cost and obligation queries, which more people are asking, sit far further down.

More demand, less competition, worse positions. That is the whole argument in one line.

## Why buyers behave this way

It is obvious once you think about how compliance work is actually bought.

Nobody wakes up wanting an asbestos survey. Something happens — a letter, a renewal, a sale, a tenant, a notice — and the first reaction is not "who can do this" but "what is this and do I have to". Only once they have established that they are obliged, and roughly what it costs, do they start looking for someone to do it.

By the time they reach the supplier-selection search, most of the decision has already been made: whether to act, when, and roughly what budget. If you only appear at that final stage, you are competing on price against everyone else who appears at that final stage, for a buyer whose expectations were set by somebody else's page.

If you appear at the first stage, you set the expectations. You are the firm that explained it. Very often the search simply ends with you, because the question has been answered and there is a form on the page.

## The two objections

**"We cannot publish prices, every job is different."**

Every job being different does not prevent a range. Nobody expects a fixed quote for an unseen building. What they need is an order of magnitude, so they know whether this is a card-payment decision or a board-approval decision.

A range, with the three or four variables that move it set out honestly, answers the question completely without committing you to anything. The page that refuses to engage with price does not keep the buyer guessing; it sends them to the competitor who did engage, and that competitor now owns the conversation.

**"Answering regulation questions is the HSE's job, not ours."**

It is also the job of whoever wants the work. A regulator's page is written to be correct. Yours can be written to be correct *and* useful: what this means for your building, what it costs, what happens next, and who can do it. That is a materially better answer to the question the person actually asked, and it is why a well-written contractor page can out-rank official guidance on the practical variants.

## Why this beats location pages

The default alternative use of the same effort is a set of location pages: the same service page rewritten for twenty towns.

That approach has aged badly. Near-duplicate pages with the place name swapped are exactly what search engines have spent a decade learning to discount, and a page with no genuine local substance has nothing to offer the reader either.

Location pages work when there is something real to say — a depot, genuine coverage, local work, local requirements, actual response times. Where that is true, build them, and build few. Where it is not, the same effort spent on one properly researched cost page will produce more.

The cost page has three advantages the location page lacks. It is unique, so it has something to rank on. It answers a question people are actually asking in volume. And it works nationally rather than for one town.

## What to do with this

Go through the last twenty enquiries. Not the ones that converted — all of them. Write down the first question each person asked.

The list will be dominated by cost and obligation questions. That list is your content plan, it is already validated by your own buyers, and almost none of it will currently be on your website.

Then check where you rank for those questions. If the answer is page three, you have exactly the problem described in [ranking without traffic](/insights/ranking-without-traffic) — and the fix is a page, not a bigger budget.
