Asbestos is the biggest search cluster in built environment compliance. It is also the one where the single most valuable change a firm can make to its website is also the simplest: stop sending two completely different buyers to the same page.

## The two buyers

**The duty holder.** Someone responsible for a non-domestic building under Regulation 4 of the Control of Asbestos Regulations, who has a standing duty to manage asbestos in it. They may have been doing nothing about it for years. What triggers the search is usually an insurance renewal, a new tenant, a change of managing agent, an incident elsewhere, or a dawning realisation that there is an obligation they have not met.

Their state of mind: uncertain, slightly anxious, often unclear on what the law actually requires of them. Their question is "what do I need to have" before it is "who can do it". Their timescale is soft — weeks, not days — and their budget is operational.

**The developer or principal contractor.** Someone about to refurbish or demolish, who needs a refurbishment and demolition survey before work can start. They know exactly what they need. They have a programme, and the survey is on the critical path.

Their state of mind: informed, transactional, impatient. Their question is "how fast and how much". Their timescale is hard — often days — and their budget is project money, which behaves completely differently from operational money.

These two people share a word. They share almost nothing else.

## What happens when they share a page

A single "Asbestos Surveys" page has to hedge. It explains both survey types, so it answers neither question first. It cannot state a turnaround time, because one buyer needs next week and the other needs tomorrow. It cannot be direct about price, because the two jobs are priced on different bases. It cannot pick a call to action, because one buyer wants to understand their obligation and the other wants a quote by close of play.

The result is a page that reads as competent and converts poorly, and which ranks weakly for everything because search engines cannot tell what it is about either.

Split it and both halves get sharper immediately.

**The management survey page** leads with the obligation. What Regulation 4 requires, who counts as a duty holder, what a management survey covers, what you get at the end, and what it typically costs. The call to action is a survey booking with a sensible lead time.

**The refurbishment and demolition page** leads with the programme. What an R&D survey involves, why it must be done before works, how intrusive it is, what the reinstatement looks like, how fast you can mobilise, and what it costs. The call to action is a quote, with a turnaround commitment attached.

Same firm, same surveyors, same vans. Two pages, two buyers, two conversations that each make sense.

## The questions underneath

Both buyers ask a lot before they enquire, and the questions divide along the same lines.

Duty holders ask about obligation and scope. Whether they need a survey at all. How often a reinspection is required. What the difference between a management survey and a refurbishment survey actually is. Whether a residential landlord is covered. What an asbestos register is and whether they are supposed to have one.

Developers and contractors ask about process and consequence. How long a survey takes. Whether the building has to be empty. What happens if asbestos is found mid-programme. Who is liable. What an air test is and when it is needed.

Each of those is a page, and each page belongs attached to the right parent. Most asbestos firms have none of them, which is why the national directories and the large consultancies hold those positions almost uncontested.

## Price the enquiry, not the survey

A management survey on a modest commercial building is a few hundred pounds. Judged on that alone, most firms conclude they cannot afford to market it.

That is the wrong unit of account. The survey is the diagnostic. What follows it, with meaningful frequency, is:

- Removal or encapsulation work, which is an order of magnitude larger.
- Air testing and clearance certification alongside the removal.
- Reinspection on a recurring cycle, because the duty to manage does not expire.
- The rest of the client's portfolio, because a duty holder with one building usually has several.

The first survey is a relationship opening, and for duty holders in particular it is a recurring one. Work out what a surveyed building is worth to you over five years, not what the survey invoice says, and the economics of competing for these searches change completely. That is the same calculation we argue for in [ranking without traffic](/insights/ranking-without-traffic) and it is the one most firms skip.

## Where the competition actually is

Search any asbestos term and the top of the page is dominated by directories, lead aggregators and a handful of national consultancies. Specialist regional firms — often the ones doing the better work — sit well below them.

That is worth understanding properly, because it changes the strategy. You are not trying to out-compete a better asbestos company. You are trying to out-compete a page that was written by someone who has never done a survey, for a buyer who would rather deal with someone who has.

That is a fight you can win on substance. A page that answers the Regulation 4 question properly, written by people who do the work, with real detail about what the survey involves and what it costs, beats a directory listing on every measure except domain authority — and domain authority stops mattering once the query gets specific enough.

Which is the argument for the long tail, and it applies here exactly as it does in [fire safety](/insights/fire-safety-head-term-long-tail).

## Start here

One page is two pages. Split it this week.

Then write the five questions you are most often asked on an initial call, one page each, each linking to whichever of the two parent pages it belongs to. That is a month of work and it will do more than a year of general brand marketing.
