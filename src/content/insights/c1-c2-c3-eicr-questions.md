There is a small, high-intent cluster of electrical searches that almost no contractor competes for, and it sits at exactly the moment a buyer is about to spend money.

It is not "electrician near me". It is people holding an EICR they do not fully understand, trying to work out what it means and what they now have to do.

## The moment

Picture the sequence. A landlord has an Electrical Installation Condition Report carried out, because the Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector regulations require one every five years. The report comes back marked **Unsatisfactory**. It contains observations coded C1, C2 and C3, written in the technical shorthand of BS 7671.

They do not know what any of that means. They know two things: the report says unsatisfactory, and there is a time limit — remedial work must be carried out within 28 days of the report, or sooner if specified.

So they search. What does C2 mean on an EICR. What is the difference between C2 and C3. Does a C3 need fixing. How long do I have to fix an unsatisfactory EICR. Who can carry out EICR remedial work.

Every one of those searches is a person who is definitely about to pay an electrician, within the month, and who does not yet know which one.

## Why nobody is there

Two reasons, and both are understandable.

**It looks like education, not sales.** Writing a page explaining what a C2 code means feels like doing the Institution of Engineering and Technology's job for them. It does not feel like marketing, so it does not get written.

**The firms who could write it best are busiest.** A qualified electrician who does EICRs daily could explain the code system better than anyone. They are also on site all day and have not thought about their website since it was built.

The result is that these queries are answered by landlord forums, general advice sites and the occasional national franchise. Rarely by a contractor who can actually do the work, which is exactly who the searcher wants to find.

## What the page should do

The temptation is to write a glossary. Resist it. A glossary ranks and converts nothing, because it answers the question and then stops.

The page needs three parts.

**Answer the question properly and immediately.** What each code means, in plain language, with what it implies for the reader. C1 — danger present, immediate action required. C2 — potentially dangerous, urgent remedial action required, and the reason the report says unsatisfactory. C3 — improvement recommended, not a failure, not required. Say it clearly and say it first.

**Then answer the question behind the question.** Which is always some version of "so what do I have to do, and when, and roughly what will it cost". The 28-day requirement. What happens if it is missed. What kind of work typical C2 observations involve. A realistic cost range for common remedials, with the caveat that it depends on the finding.

**Then make the next step obvious and small.** Not "contact us". Something shaped like the moment: send us your EICR and we will quote the remedials. That is a specific, low-effort action that matches exactly what the person wants to do, and it hands you a document that tells you precisely what the job is before you have spoken to them.

That last point is worth dwelling on. An enquiry that arrives with the report attached is pre-qualified to a degree almost no other trade enquiry is. You know the installation, the faults, the urgency and the property. You can quote accurately without a site visit. That is a dramatically better lead than a form that says "need an electrician".

## The strategic point

Electrical testing and electrical remedial work are usually sold by the same firms, and usually marketed as one thing. They are two different sales to two different states of mind.

Testing is bought ahead of a deadline, often reluctantly, and competes largely on price. It is a commodity and it is priced like one.

Remedial work is bought *after* a problem has been identified by someone the buyer already trusts, under time pressure, and competes on availability and confidence far more than price.

The second is better business. The path into it is being the firm that explained the report — which, in a market where the incumbent tester has already quoted, is a genuine opening. A landlord unhappy with their tester's remedial quote is actively looking for an alternative, and the only way they find you is if you rank for the thing they are typing.

## It is small, and that is fine

This is not a large cluster. Across the brands we run, electrical and EICR queries are one of the smaller groups, well behind asbestos or fire.

What makes it worth the afternoon it takes to write properly is intent density. Nearly everyone searching these phrases has a report in their hand and a deadline on it. The conversion rate on that traffic is unlike anything you will get from a generic electrician search, and the competition for it is close to nil.

Small, uncontested and about to spend money is a good combination. It is the same argument as [TM44](/insights/tm44-the-inspection-nobody-markets) and the same one behind the whole cohort: [the demand is already there and nobody is answering it](/insights/ranking-without-traffic).
