Planned preventative maintenance behaves differently from every other kind of trade enquiry, and most firms market it as though it does not.

A reactive job is a transaction. Something is broken, someone fixes it, both parties move on. A PPM contract is a multi-year commitment covering a building or a portfolio, awarded after a comparison process, usually by displacing an incumbent. The buyer is not booking work. They are choosing a supplier.

Everything about the marketing should follow from that, and usually none of it does.

## The searches are low in number and high in value

PPM queries sit well down the volume table compared with asbestos or fire. "Planned preventative maintenance", "PPM survey", "PPM schedule", "hard FM contract" and their variants are a modest cluster.

They are also, per enquiry, among the most valuable things in the built environment. A single PPM contract on a commercial portfolio can be worth more than a year of reactive work. Judging this cluster by volume is the standard mistake; judged by pipeline value it is near the top.

Which means the normal advice — chase volume, optimise cost per lead — is exactly wrong here. A handful of enquiries a quarter, properly handled, is the whole programme.

## Who is searching, and why now

PPM enquiries almost never come from a standing start. They come from a trigger:

- A contract is up for renewal and procurement is required to test the market.
- Service under the incumbent has slipped and someone has had enough.
- A building or portfolio has been acquired and needs covering.
- An insurer, auditor or compliance review has flagged that planned maintenance is not documented.
- A facilities manager is new in post and reviewing inherited arrangements.

In almost every case there is an incumbent. That is the single most important fact about this market and the one most PPM pages ignore entirely. The page is not introducing the concept of maintenance to someone who has never considered it. It is making the case for change to someone who already has a supplier and is weighing the hassle of switching.

So the page needs to address switching directly: how mobilisation works, how you take over an existing schedule, what happens to asset registers and historical records, how long the handover takes, and what the first ninety days look like. Firms write about their capabilities. Buyers at this moment are worried about disruption.

## The survey is the product

The commercial mechanism in PPM is the survey, and it is routinely mispriced.

A PPM survey walks the building, inventories the assets, assesses condition, and produces a maintenance schedule with frequencies and a cost. Done properly it takes real time, and firms either charge too little for it or give it away and resent it.

Both miss the point. The survey is not the sale. It is the qualification, the specification and the switching cost all at once:

- It tells you whether the contract is worth having before you price it.
- It produces the schedule the contract is built on, written by you, in your format.
- It gives the buyer a document that makes you the obvious incumbent for the tender you just wrote.

A free or near-free survey on a qualified portfolio is cheap customer acquisition. On an unqualified one it is a wasted day. Which is why the qualification has to happen in the form, before the visit.

## What the form must ask

Most trade enquiry forms ask for name, number and "how can we help". For PPM that is useless, because you cannot tell a single-site enquiry from a forty-site portfolio.

Ask the things that decide whether this is worth a survey:

- How many sites, and roughly what type and size
- What is covered now, and by whom
- When the current arrangement ends
- What has prompted the review
- Who else is involved in the decision

Five questions. Each one is a qualification gate, and together they let you separate the portfolio enquiry that justifies dropping everything from the single-unit enquiry that does not. There is more on building forms that filter rather than just collect in [quotation forms that qualify](/insights/quotation-forms-that-qualify).

Buyers at this stage do not resent a longer form. A procurement-minded facilities manager expects to give you information before you quote, and a form that asks sensible questions signals competence. The friction argument applies to a homeowner booking a survey, not to someone awarding a contract.

## Speed still decides it

It is tempting to assume that a long procurement cycle means there is no hurry. The opposite is true. A buyer testing the market contacts three or four suppliers in one sitting. The one who responds first shapes the specification, and the specification shapes who wins.

A same-day response to a PPM enquiry is worth more than a same-day response to a reactive job, because the prize is larger and the window is narrower. We made the general case for this in [the first five minutes](/insights/the-first-five-minutes); on contract work the effect is stronger, not weaker.

## The short version

Low volume, high value, incumbent to displace, survey as the hinge. Build the page for a switching decision rather than a booking, qualify hard in the form so the survey goes where it is worth going, and answer fast.

PPM also belongs to a broader pattern worth understanding if you sell anything on a contract rather than by the job — [marketing a service nobody searches for twice](/insights/marketing-a-contract-not-a-job).
