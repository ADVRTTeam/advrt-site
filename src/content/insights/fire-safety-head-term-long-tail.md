Fire protection is the broadest search landscape in built environment compliance, and the one where the gap between how buyers search and how firms present themselves is widest.

It is also, commercially, the best-shaped work in the sector. A fire risk assessment is rarely a single transaction. It produces findings, the findings produce remedial work, the remedial work produces a service schedule, and the schedule renews. The enquiry at the front of that chain is worth a multiple of the assessment fee — which changes what you can afford to pay for it, and how hard it is worth working to win it.

## The shape of the demand

Run the queries for any fire protection business and you find the same structure.

There is one dominant head term — "fire risk assessment" and its close variants — which carries more volume than everything else combined and attracts every national provider, every directory and every franchise. Competing there directly, from a standing start, is a long and expensive project.

Behind it sits an extraordinarily wide tail. Hundreds of distinct phrasings, each individually small, covering:

- **Servicing and interval questions.** How often extinguishers need servicing, when alarms need testing, how frequently an assessment must be reviewed.
- **Specification questions.** Which extinguisher for which risk, what class of fire, what emergency lighting a particular building needs.
- **Obligation questions.** Who the responsible person is, what the Fire Safety Order requires, what changed under the Building Safety Act.
- **Cost questions.** What an assessment costs, what a service contract costs, what remedial work typically runs to.

Individually, none of these looks worth a page. Collectively they are the larger half of the market, and almost nobody is competing for them, because every firm's instinct is to build one page called "Fire Risk Assessments" and point everything at it.

## Why the tail is the better target

Three reasons, and they compound.

**It is winnable.** The head term is contested by organisations with budgets and domain authority you are not going to beat this year. A question like "how often should a fire risk assessment be reviewed" is contested by almost nobody with a commercial interest in answering it well. You can own it inside a quarter.

**The intent is often better, not worse.** A buyer asking how often an assessment must be reviewed is a responsible person who already has an obligation and is working out whether they are currently in breach. That is a warmer prospect than someone comparing providers, because they have not yet decided to shop around. You get to be the first firm they speak to rather than the third quote.

**It builds the authority that eventually wins the head term.** A site with thirty genuinely useful pages on fire safety obligations is a different proposition to a search engine than a site with one. The tail is not a consolation prize for failing to rank on the head term. It is the route to ranking on the head term.

## How to structure the site

The instinct is to mirror the service list: assessments, extinguishers, alarms, emergency lighting, passive protection. That is a reasonable spine, and it is not enough on its own, because it answers "what do you sell" rather than "what did I ask".

Layer the obligation questions over the service pages.

**One page per service**, as the commercial destination. Each one specific, each one with its own enquiry route, each one written for the buyer of that service rather than for a general audience. A facilities manager renewing an extinguisher contract and a responsible person commissioning a first assessment are different people with different anxieties.

**One page per real question**, feeding into them. Short, direct, genuinely answering the thing asked, and linking into the relevant service page as the obvious next step. The page that explains review intervals should make it effortless to book the review.

**Separate the buyers, not just the services.** Fire work is bought by duty holders, facilities managers, managing agents and main contractors, and they do not search the same way or care about the same things. Where two buyers want the same service for different reasons, that is often two pages, not one. There is a fuller argument for that in [how many pages a multi-trade firm actually needs](/insights/how-many-pages-does-a-multi-trade-firm-need).

## Answer the cost question

Fire protection firms resist publishing prices harder than almost any other trade, with two stated reasons: every building is different, and competitors will undercut.

The first is true and does not matter. Nobody expects a fixed price for an assessment on an unseen building. They expect to know whether this is a two-hundred-pound thing or a two-thousand-pound thing, because that determines whether they need to raise it with someone. A range, with the variables that move it set out honestly, answers the question without committing you to anything.

The second is mostly imagined. Competitors already know your pricing. Buyers who cannot find it leave and ask the firm that published theirs. The page you did not write is not protecting your margin; it is sending the enquiry to someone else.

## What the first enquiry is actually worth

This is the part worth working out before you decide what to spend.

Take the assessment fee. Add the proportion of assessments that convert into remedial work, and what that work averages. Add the proportion that convert into a service contract, and the contract value. Then multiply the contract by its realistic life, because a fire service contract that renews for five years is five years of revenue from one enquiry.

Most fire protection firms we have worked with discover that the lifetime value of a first assessment enquiry is somewhere between five and fifteen times the assessment fee. That number is the one that makes the marketing maths work, and it is almost never the number people have in their head when they decide a lead is too expensive.

If you want to run that calculation against your own figures, the [fire protection page](/fire-protection) has a working version of it.

## The short version

The head term is a long war. The tail is available now, it converts at least as well, and winning it is how you eventually win the head term anyway.

Go and list every question a buyer has asked you on the phone in the last month. That list is your content plan, and almost none of it is on your website.
