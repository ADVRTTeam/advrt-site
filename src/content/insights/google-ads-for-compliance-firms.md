Compliance is the easiest sector in the built environment to run paid search for, and the one where most accounts are set up worst. Both facts have the same cause: the intent is so obvious that nobody thinks hard about it.

Someone searching "EICR Leeds" is not researching. They are not comparing philosophies of electrical safety. They have a deadline, a property and a need for a certificate, and they are going to book with whoever makes that easy in the next ten minutes.

## The search is the decision

Treat that urgency as the design brief for the whole campaign.

A buyer on a deadline wants four things, in this order: can you do it, where, how much, how soon. Anything on the page that delays those answers is costing you the booking. Clear prices, a turnaround time, a coverage area and a way to book in a few taps on a phone.

This is why compliance pages that read like brochures underperform pages that read like a booking form with context. The persuasion already happened. It happened in the regulation.

## One discipline, one area, one campaign

The structural mistake is lumping everything into a single campaign because it is all "compliance".

An EPC and a fire risk assessment share almost nothing. Different buyers, different triggers, different values, wildly different margins. Pooling them means one budget, one set of bids and no ability to push the work you actually want.

- **EPCs** are volume at speed. Landlords, agents, sellers, MEES deadlines. Won on price, availability and how fast the job gets into a diary.
- **EICRs** are recurring local demand on a five-yearly cycle, with a natural route into remedial work that is worth more than the inspection.
- **Gas safety** is annual and repeatable. The same landlords come back every year if you remind them.
- **Fire risk assessments** are higher-value B2B work with a longer conversation, qualified by premises type, size and occupancy.
- **Asbestos and legionella** are specialist long-tail searches from buyers who know exactly what they need and will use the right words for it.

Split them out and each one reports separately. Within two months you can see which disciplines are carrying the account and which are quietly eating budget, and move spend accordingly.

> Pooling your disciplines into one campaign is choosing not to know which of them makes you money.

## Landlords and agents are a different sale

The highest-value thing in a compliance account is usually not a job. It is a relationship.

A homeowner needs one EPC, maybe twice in a decade. A letting agent with two hundred properties on their books needs certificates continuously, forever. Both might arrive through the same search, and treating them identically wastes the second one.

So run them as separate journeys. Agent and portfolio-landlord pages should lead with volume handling, account management, invoicing and reliability, not with a single-job price. Capture portfolio size on the form. Flag those enquiries for a same-day call from someone who can talk commercially.

One agent account won properly is worth more than a month of one-off bookings, and it does not churn when your ad budget changes.

## On price-shopping

Every compliance firm says the same thing: the market is brutal on price. It is true, and it is less decisive than it feels.

Being cheapest is one way to win. Being easiest is another, and it is more defensible. Clear pricing, honest availability, an instant quote where the work is fixed-price, and a call back in minutes rather than hours. Plenty of buyers will pay a little more for certainty when they are against a deadline.

What loses the job is ambiguity: no price, no availability, a contact form that promises a response within two working days. By then they have booked someone else.
