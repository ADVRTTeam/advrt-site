We run lead generation brands in the built environment with our own money. Not case studies for clients — brands we own, where a bad month costs us rather than someone else. That gives us twelve months of search data on asbestos, fire safety, air conditioning, legionella, electrical testing and planned maintenance, and one pattern repeats across all of them.

The demand is there. The sites are eligible to answer it. And they get almost no clicks, because they sit far enough down the results that nobody scrolls to them.

That is not a small problem dressed up. It is the single most common reason a specialist contractor's website does nothing, and it is almost always misdiagnosed.

## "We rank for that" is not an answer

Ask a damp proofing firm, a fire safety company or an asbestos consultancy whether they rank for their main service and most will say yes. Check it and they are right — somewhere between position fifteen and position eighty.

Both are "ranking". Only one of them is a business.

The click-through curve in organic search is brutally top-heavy. The first result takes a large share of the clicks. By the bottom of page one you are into low single digits. On page two it rounds to nothing, and page three is indistinguishable from not existing. A page sitting at position forty for a query with genuine commercial demand is not an underperforming asset. It is an absence with a URL.

This matters more in the trades than almost anywhere else, because trade queries are not browsing queries. Someone searching for a fire risk assessment or an asbestos survey is not reading around a subject. They have a building, an obligation and a date. They will click two or three results, fill in one form and stop. If you are not in those two or three, the search never happened as far as you are concerned.

## Why so many trade sites rank badly for the right words

The pattern we see is consistent enough to be predictable.

**The page was written for a different job.** A services page that lists eleven disciplines is trying to be a brochure, a credibility signal and a sales document at once. Search engines read it as a page about nothing in particular. It will pick up rankings for long, odd phrases that happen to match a sentence in the middle of it, and rank weakly for all of them.

**The query is a question and the page is a statement.** Buyers in compliance-driven trades ask questions before they ask for a quote. Is this a legal requirement? How often does it need doing? What does it cost? Most trade sites answer none of these, because the firm already knows the answers and has forgotten that the buyer does not.

**Nobody picked the target.** This is the big one. The pages rank by accident. No one sat down, looked at what buyers type, and decided which phrases this firm intends to own. So the site competes everywhere and wins nowhere.

**The competition is thinner than anyone believes.** In several of the disciplines we track, the firms ranking above our brands are directories, national franchises and content aggregators — not specialist operators. The actual specialists, the ones who do the work well, are below them. That is an opportunity, not a verdict.

## The demand is not where most firms look for it

When a trade firm does decide to take search seriously, the instinct is almost always to chase the obvious transactional phrase — the service plus a town. "Asbestos survey Manchester." "Fire risk assessment Leeds."

Those terms are real, and they are also the most contested, most expensive and smallest part of the picture.

Across the compliance disciplines we run, two kinds of query consistently outnumber the "who do I hire" searches, by a wide margin:

**Price and cost questions.** What does a legionella risk assessment cost. How much is a commercial EPC. What should I expect to pay for an asbestos refurbishment and demolition survey. These are buyers in the room with the budget conversation.

**Obligation questions.** Is legionella testing a legal requirement. How often does a fire risk assessment need reviewing. Do I need a TM44 inspection. These are buyers who do not yet know they are obliged — which means they do not yet know they need you.

Both cluster rank far worse than the "near me" terms on the sites we track, which tells you something useful: more people are asking, and fewer firms are answering. We wrote about that split in more detail in [why price and regulation questions beat "near me"](/insights/price-and-regulation-beat-near-me).

## What a fix actually looks like

It is rarely a budget problem and it is almost never a "more content" problem. It is a targeting problem, and the work is specific.

**Pick the queries first, then build the page.** Not the other way round. Decide the handful of phrases this firm intends to rank for, confirm they have genuine demand and genuine commercial intent, and write a page whose entire job is to be the best answer to one of them.

**One page, one question, one buyer.** A page that answers "how much does an asbestos survey cost" is a different page from one that answers "do I need an asbestos survey before refurbishment", and both are different from the page that sells your survey service. Trying to merge them produces a page that loses all three. There is more on this in [how many pages a multi-trade firm actually needs](/insights/how-many-pages-does-a-multi-trade-firm-need).

**Answer the question in the first screen.** Not after the company history. If the query is a cost question, there should be a number or a range near the top, with the honest caveats underneath. Firms resist this because pricing feels like a competitive disclosure. It is not. It is the reason the buyer clicked, and withholding it sends them back to the results page to click the firm that did answer.

**Give the page somewhere to go.** An answer page that ends in a full stop converts nothing. It needs an obvious, low-friction next step that matches where the buyer is — usually a specific quote request for that specific service, not a general contact form.

**Then measure the position, not the traffic.** When a page is new, traffic is a lagging indicator and a demoralising one. Position is the leading indicator. A page that moves from forty to fifteen has done most of the hard work even though the clicks have barely moved, because the move from fifteen to five is far shorter than the one it has already made.

## The honest version of the opportunity

It would be easy to turn this into a pitch about untapped markets. It is more useful to be precise about what the data does and does not say.

It does not say these are enormous markets. Individually, most of these query clusters are modest. A single compliance discipline in a single region is a handful of searches a day, not a flood.

What it says is that the demand is **real, repeated, high-intent and almost entirely uncontested by the firms best placed to serve it.** A handful of searches a day, for work that carries a four-figure value and a renewal cycle, converted at a normal rate, is a very good year. And the competition for those searches is, in several disciplines, directories and franchises rather than specialists.

That is a far better position to start from than a crowded market with sophisticated competitors. It is also a position with a shelf life, because it closes the moment the better operators notice.

## Where to start

If you take one thing from this: go and check where you actually rank for the five phrases you most want to own. Not whether you rank. Where.

If the answer is page one, your problem is conversion and you should be reading about [forms that qualify](/insights/quotation-forms-that-qualify) and [response speed](/insights/the-first-five-minutes) instead.

If the answer is page three, you have a page problem, and no amount of ad spend will fix the underlying asset. Fix the page. The demand is already there, waiting, and nobody else is answering it.
