There is no shortage of agencies willing to run Google Ads for a contracting business. There is a real shortage of agencies that have done it well for one, and from the outside the two groups are indistinguishable. Same case studies, same logos, same promise of qualified leads.

We sell this service, so read what follows knowing that. The questions below are the ones we would want to be asked, and they are the ones that have most often separated a good appointment from an expensive one in the conversations we have had with firms who came to us after a bad experience elsewhere. Put them to anyone you are considering, including us.

## What do you already know about my buyer?

Not your industry. Your buyer. A fit-out contractor sells to office tenants, project managers and landlords. A groundworks firm sells to main contractors and developers. A compliance contractor sells to duty holders who are on a deadline. These people search differently, decide differently and are triggered by different events, and an agency that has not thought about that will build you a campaign for "contractors" and wonder why it fills your inbox with homeowners.

Listen for specifics. Which phrases convert in your trade and which attract clicks that never become jobs. What a buyer asks before they are ready to request a quote. What a form can ask before completion rates fall. If the answers are generic, the agency intends to find all of this out on your budget. There is nothing wrong with that as long as you know it is the deal. We have written about [why we think that cost should sit with the agency](/insights/why-we-test-on-our-own-money-first) rather than the client, but the honest minimum is that they tell you.

## Have you spent your own money in this sector?

A sharper version of the same question. An agency that has run its own campaigns in the built environment, on its own spend, has learned things it could not have learned any other way, because it handled the enquiries and felt the cost of the ones that went nowhere. An agency that has only ever managed client budgets has learned what clients let it learn.

Most will say no, and that is a legitimate answer. The point is that a no should be followed by a credible explanation of how they will shorten the learning curve, not by a change of subject.

## Who owns the account?

This is the question that costs firms the most when they do not ask it.

The ad account, the landing pages, the tracking configuration and the conversion data are the pipeline. If they sit in the agency's accounts, then the day you leave, the pipeline leaves with them. The campaign history that makes the bidding efficient, the audience lists, the negative keyword lists built over months of waste, the pages that convert: gone, or held hostage during a notice period.

The right answer is that everything is built in accounts you own, with the agency given access, and that the access can be revoked by you at any time without breaking anything. Some agencies will say their own account is more efficient. It is, for them. The same logic applies to the pages your ads land on, and to the phone numbers the ads display. Ask whose name is on each.

## Where will the traffic land?

If the answer is your homepage, keep looking.

A homepage is written for everyone and converts nobody in particular. Paid search works when the page matches the search, which means a page per service, written for the buyer of that service, with one clear next step. An agency that intends to send paid traffic to your existing site without building anything is planning to optimise the ads and ignore the thing the ads lead to, which is where most of the result is decided. We set out the case in [one page per service](/insights/one-page-per-service), and it is the single biggest difference between campaigns that pay back and campaigns that do not.

Ask who builds the pages, who owns them, and whether they sit on your domain.

## How will you know which enquiries became jobs?

A report that shows clicks, impressions and form submissions is a description of activity. It tells you nothing about whether the activity produced paid work.

The agency should be able to describe how a won job gets connected back to the search, the ad and the page that produced it, and how that information is fed back into the platform so it can optimise towards revenue rather than volume. In the built environment that has to include phone calls, because the best enquiries ring rather than type. If call tracking is not part of the proposal, a large share of your results will be invisible to both of you. The mechanics are set out in [attribution without the headache](/insights/attribution-without-the-headache).

Then ask the follow-up: what will you do with the enquiries that do not convert? An agency that wants to see your lost enquiries is one that intends to learn from them.

## What number will you optimise for?

Cost per lead is the number every agency reports because it is the number every agency can control. Cost per won job is the number your business runs on, and the gap between them is your conversion rate, which depends on the quality of what the campaign sends you.

An agency optimising for cheap leads will find them, because there are always cheaper leads. They will be homeowners, students, people in the wrong county and people who wanted a different trade. There is a longer version of the argument in [what a surveyor lead actually costs](/insights/what-a-surveyor-lead-actually-costs), and the arithmetic is the same in every trade.

## Do you work for my competitors?

A reasonable agency will have more than one client in a sector. That is how they learn it. But two firms bidding on the same phrases in the same patch, managed by the same agency, is a conflict that nobody can manage in both clients' favour. One of them is being optimised against the other.

Ask directly whether they work with anyone doing your work in your area, and what their policy is if they are approached by one. The answer tells you whether they have thought about it.

## What are the first thirty days?

A campaign that starts spending in week one, before any pages are built, any tracking is configured or any keyword research has been shown to you, is a campaign that will spend its first month finding out what your money could have told it for free. A credible first month is mostly build: pages, tracking, call recording, form logic, search term research, negative lists. Spend follows, and it follows small.

Be suspicious of any plan that reaches full budget before anything has been measured.

## How do you charge, and what does that make you want?

Every pricing model pushes the agency somewhere. None of them is dishonest. All of them should be understood.

**A percentage of spend** means the agency earns more when you spend more. It is simple, and it rewards growing the budget whether or not the extra spend is efficient.

**A flat retainer** removes that incentive and replaces it with a different one: the agency earns the same whether the account is worked hard or left alone. Ask what work the retainer buys, in hours or in deliverables, and how you will see it.

**Per lead** sounds like the agency is sharing your risk, and sometimes it is. It also rewards volume over quality, which is the problem described two sections ago, and it usually means the agency owns the account and the pages, because they are selling you the output rather than the asset. Look hard at whether what is being offered is an agency relationship or a lead portal with a nicer website. [Comparison sites and shared leads](/insights/comparison-sites-vs-your-own-pipeline) are a legitimate product, but they are a different product.

**Performance or hybrid models** tie part of the fee to results. These work when the result is defined as something you care about, such as qualified enquiries or won jobs, and when the definition is written down. They fail when "a lead" turns out to mean a form submission from anyone at all.

There is no right model. There is a right question, which is: when this agency does well for itself, is it also doing well for me?

## What happens when I leave?

Ask about the minimum term, the notice period and the handover. The answer you want is a short minimum, a stated notice period, and a clear statement that every asset is already yours and nothing needs to be handed over because nothing needs to move.

An agency confident in its work does not need to lock you in. One that does need to is telling you something about how it expects the relationship to go.

## The warning signs, briefly

One of these is a question to ask. Two or three together is a reason to walk away.

- Guaranteed rankings, guaranteed lead numbers, or guaranteed cost per lead, before any data exists.
- Campaigns run in the agency's own ad account, or landing pages hosted on the agency's domain.
- Reports built on clicks, impressions and form fills, with no path to won work.
- No mention of phone tracking in a trade where buyers call.
- A plan that reaches full spend in week one.
- Case studies that name a result but not a sector, or a sector but not a result.
- Reluctance to say whether they work for your competitors.
- A pricing model whose incentives they cannot, or will not, explain.

## What a good answer sounds like

It sounds specific. It names your buyer, not your industry. It describes the first month as a build. It puts every asset in your name and says so without being asked. It measures won jobs and admits that doing so is harder than measuring clicks. It explains its own pricing incentive honestly. And it is comfortable with the possibility that after three months of real data, the numbers might tell you to stop.

Most of this is not about skill with the platform. It is about whether the agency understands that it is being hired to build something you will own, in a market it has taken the trouble to understand. The firms we have seen do well with paid search, in [contracting](/contractors) and across the [wider built environment](/built-environment), are the ones who asked these questions first and chose on the answers.
