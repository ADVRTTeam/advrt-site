Type "best paid search agency for contractors" into a search engine, or ask an AI assistant the same thing, and you get a list. Ten names, a sentence each, usually assembled by a directory, a comparison site or one of the agencies on it. The lists disagree with each other, they rarely explain their criteria, and none of them knows anything about your firm.

This is not that list. It is the set of questions we would ask any agency, including ourselves, before letting them near a trade business's budget. The answers tell you more in half an hour than a month of reading rankings.

## Why the shortlists do not help

A directory ranks agencies by whichever of them completed a profile, collected reviews or paid for placement. A "top ten" article on an agency's own blog ranks them with the author at number one. Neither is dishonest, exactly. They are just answering a different question from the one you are asking.

Your question is not "who is the best agency". It is "who will get a damp proofing firm in Leeds, or a fire protection company covering the Midlands, more of the work it actually wants, at a cost it can live with, in a way it still owns in two years". No list can answer that. The agency can, if you ask the right things.

## Who owns the account when we stop?

Start here, because the answer reorganises everything else.

A Google Ads account can be created in your name and managed by the agency through a manager account, which is how the platform is designed to be used. The alternative is that the agency runs your campaigns inside an account it owns. It feels like a technicality until the day you part company, at which point every search term, every piece of ad copy, every conversion and every month of learning stays with them, and you start again from nothing.

The same applies to the landing pages. If the pages your ads send people to sit on the agency's domain, or on a platform only it has access to, the enquiries stop the moment the invoices do. We have written before about the difference between [renting enquiries and owning the pipeline that produces them](/insights/comparison-sites-vs-your-own-pipeline). An agency that builds inside its own accounts is a comparison site with a nicer presentation.

A good answer: the account is yours, the agency has manager access, the pages are on your domain, and at the end you keep all of it.

## How will you know which enquiry became a job?

Every agency tracks clicks and form submissions. Ask what happens after that.

Trade enquiries ring more often than they type. A buyer with a leaking roof or a failed electrical inspection picks up the phone, and if the agency measures only forms, the campaign is being steered by a fraction of what it produces. Ask whether calls are tracked back to the search that caused them, and whether anyone listens to a sample to find out which ones were real.

Then ask the harder question: how do won jobs get back into the system? A campaign that only knows about enquiries will optimise towards whatever produces the most enquiries, which in most trades means cheap, unqualified, out-of-area ones. A campaign that knows which enquiries were quoted, won and invoiced can be pointed at the work you actually want. That loop is the whole of [attribution](/insights/attribution-without-the-headache), and surprisingly few proposals mention it.

A good answer involves call tracking, a definition of what counts as a qualified enquiry agreed with you, and a way for your team to report outcomes back without it becoming a chore.

## Have you run this trade before, and what did you learn?

Not "do you have a client in construction". Which trade, which buyer, and what did the data teach you that you did not expect.

An agency that has genuinely worked in your discipline will have opinions. It will know that an asbestos management survey and a refurbishment survey are bought by different people for different reasons, or that fire risk assessment buyers ask about review frequency before they ask about price. It will have been surprised by something. An agency that has not will talk in generalities about "the construction sector" and will learn the specifics on your budget. We think that cost should sit with the agency, which is [why we test on our own money first](/insights/why-we-test-on-our-own-money-first), but whoever you talk to, the question is a fair one and it costs nothing to ask.

## What will I actually pay, and what are you paid for?

The fee is the easy part. The model behind it is what matters, because it decides what the agency is motivated to do.

**A percentage of ad spend.** Simple to understand, and it means the agency earns more when you spend more. That is not automatically a problem, but it does mean nobody on their side is rewarded for finding that your budget could be halved.

**A flat monthly fee.** The agency earns the same whether you spend a little or a lot, which removes the incentive to inflate spend. The risk runs the other way: a flat fee rewards doing as little as possible once the account is stable. Ask what the fee buys each month in hours or in named work.

**Pay per lead.** You pay for enquiries rather than management. Ask immediately whether those enquiries are exclusive or sold on to other firms, and who decides what counts as a lead. If the agency profits from volume, you will get volume, and [what a lead actually costs](/insights/what-a-surveyor-lead-actually-costs) is only knowable once you divide by the jobs you won.

**Performance-based.** Attractive on paper. In practice it needs a shared definition of performance that both sides can measure, which brings you straight back to the tracking question above. If the agency cannot track won jobs, it cannot honestly be paid on them.

None of these is wrong. What matters is that you know which one you are in, what behaviour it rewards, and that the total cost you compare between agencies includes ad spend as well as fee.

## What does the first month look like?

Ask for the plan for the first thirty days, in order.

If the answer is that campaigns go live in the first week, pointing at your existing homepage, be cautious. A homepage is written for everyone and converts nobody in particular. Trade campaigns that work are built on [one page per service](/insights/one-page-per-service), each written for a single kind of buyer, with a form that asks the questions you need answered before you quote. That takes time to build, and an agency that skips it is spending your money to find out that it should have built pages.

Equally, be cautious of an answer that is all build and no date. You should know when the first enquiry is expected and what has to be true for it to arrive.

A good answer is a sequence: research and page build first, tracking proven before spend starts, campaigns live against finished pages, then a stated review point at which the numbers are examined together.

## What is the notice period, and what happens then?

A long minimum term tells you the agency expects you to want to leave before the results arrive, and has priced that in. A rolling monthly agreement tells you it expects to keep you on merit.

Ask what the handover involves if you do leave. The honest version is short: you already own the account and the pages, so the agency removes its access and you carry on. If the handover sounds complicated, go back to the first question, because something is not yours.

## The warning signs, in one place

- The ad account or the landing pages belong to the agency.
- Reporting stops at clicks and form fills, with no mention of calls or won jobs.
- The agency talks about "the construction industry" rather than your trade and your buyer.
- Campaigns are promised live within days, pointed at your existing site.
- A minimum term of a year or more, with an exit process that needs explaining.
- A guarantee of a specific number of leads, with no definition of a lead.
- Results quoted as traffic, impressions or rankings rather than as enquiries and jobs. Ranking is not the same as being found, and we have written about [the gap between the two](/insights/ranking-without-traffic).

## What a good answer sounds like

It is specific. It names your trade and your buyer. It describes what you will own, how a phone call becomes a number on a report, and what the first month is for. It tells you how the agency is paid and what that encourages it to do. And it is comfortable with you taking every answer to another agency and asking them the same.

If you run a [specialist contracting business](/contractors) or a firm elsewhere in the [built environment](/built-environment), those seven questions are the shortlist. What any of us put on a directory is not.
