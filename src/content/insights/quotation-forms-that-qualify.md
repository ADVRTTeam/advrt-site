There is a received wisdom about forms that is half right and causes real damage. It goes: shorter forms convert better, so ask for as little as possible.

The first part is true. A three-field form will get more submissions than a twelve-field one. The problem is what those submissions are worth, and what happens to the eight hours a week your team spends turning them into something useful.

## A name and a number is not a lead

Picture the enquiry a minimal form produces. Name, phone number, maybe "I need a survey".

You cannot price it. You cannot judge whether you want it. You cannot tell if the property is in your patch, whether the budget is realistic, or whether this is urgent or idle curiosity. So someone calls to find out, and that call is pure cost whether the job is any good or not.

Now count what happens across a month of those. The form did not qualify anything. It just moved the qualifying onto your team, one phone call at a time, and called it a conversion.

> A form that asks nothing has not captured a lead. It has booked you a phone call to find out whether you wanted one.

## Two stages gets you both

The resolution is not a longer form. It is a form in two parts.

**Stage one** asks for the minimum: name, email, phone. That is the commitment point, and keeping it light is why conversion holds up. Critically, this is submitted and recorded. If they go no further, you still have the enquiry.

**Stage two** asks the qualifying questions, on the next screen, once they are already invested: property type, approximate value, location, timescale, scope, budget range.

Completion rates on stage two are high, because of simple psychology: having started something, people finish it. And the ones who abandon stage two have still given you stage one, so nothing is lost that a short form would have caught.

What arrives in your inbox is an enquiry you can price without a phone call. The call you then make is a quote, not an interview.

## Ask about budget. Properly.

This is where firms get nervous, and the nervousness costs them.

The fear is that asking about budget scares people off. Sometimes it does, and those are mostly people who were never going to meet your minimum fee. An architecture practice with a £15,000 minimum does not benefit from enquiries about a £2,000 job, however pleasant the conversation.

The trick is in the framing. A range, not a number. "Roughly what budget do you have in mind?" with four or five bands and a "not sure yet" option. The band tells you enough to triage, the escape hatch keeps the genuinely early-stage enquiries in play, and nobody is made to feel interrogated.

## Make every question earn its place

The discipline that keeps a form honest: for each field, ask what you would do differently based on the answer.

- **Changes your price?** Keep it. Property type, size, scope, number of delegates.
- **Changes whether you take the job?** Keep it. Location, budget band, timescale.
- **Changes who calls them, or how fast?** Keep it. Portfolio size, group booking, deadline.
- **Nice to know?** Cut it. It is costing you completions and buying nothing.

Most forms in this industry can lose a third of their fields and gain a better enquiry, simply by replacing the questions that flatter the business with the ones that price the job.

And a last practical note: make it work on a phone. Most first contact in the built environment now happens on mobile, often standing in the property being discussed. A form that needs pinching and zooming is a form that gets abandoned, however good the questions are.
