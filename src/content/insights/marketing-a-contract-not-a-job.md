A large share of the best work in the built environment is sold as a contract rather than a job. Fire equipment servicing. Water hygiene monitoring. Planned maintenance. Washroom services. Grounds and cleaning. Testing programmes on a statutory cycle.

It is excellent business — recurring, predictable, high retention. It is also marketed badly almost everywhere, because the people marketing it apply a model built for one-off work.

## The behaviour inverts

For job work, the funnel is familiar. Demand recurs. The same buyer searches again next time something breaks. Volume is reasonably steady, you can test and iterate, and a lost enquiry is annoying rather than fatal.

Contract work does none of that.

**The buyer searches once.** They review the market, choose a supplier, sign, and then do not search again for three, five, sometimes ten years. There is no second chance with that buyer in any meaningful timeframe.

**Volume is low and lumpy.** You might see a handful of genuine enquiries a quarter. That is not enough data to optimise against in the usual way, and treating it as a volume channel will lead you to the wrong conclusions.

**There is almost always an incumbent.** Very few buildings have no arrangement at all. The enquiry you receive is a switching decision, not a first purchase, and the competitor you have to beat is not the other firm bidding — it is the inertia of staying put.

**The value is enormous relative to the enquiry.** One contract can be worth more than a year of reactive work.

Low volume, high value, one shot, incumbent to displace. Every strategic decision follows from that.

## What the page has to do

Most contract-service pages describe the service. The buyer already knows what the service is — they are currently buying it from someone else. What they do not know is what changing would be like.

**Address the switch head-on.** How mobilisation works. What happens to existing records, asset registers and schedules. How long handover takes. What the first ninety days look like. Whether there is a gap in coverage. Who their point of contact is. These are the real objections and almost no page answers them.

**Be specific about what is included.** Contract buyers have been burned by scope ambiguity. A clear statement of what is in, what is extra, and what triggers a variation does more for trust than any amount of reassurance copy.

**Talk about the exit.** Counter-intuitive, and powerful. A firm confident enough to state its notice period and its handover obligations is signalling that it expects to be kept on merit. A buyer who has been locked into a bad contract reads that very clearly.

**Make reporting visible.** Compliance contracts are bought partly as evidence. Showing what the documentation looks like — the certificates, the schedules, the records they will be able to produce at audit — speaks directly to why they are buying.

## The searches that exist

Because the same buyer rarely searches twice, the volume is low. But the cluster that does exist is unusually valuable, and it breaks into three.

**Renewal and tender.** Someone whose contract is ending and who has to test the market. Highest intent of anything in the trade, and the clearest commercial moment there is.

**Dissatisfaction.** Searches that reveal a problem with the incumbent — a service that has not been attended, a missed visit, a failed audit. These buyers are not comparing; they are leaving. Being findable here is enormously valuable and essentially uncontested.

**Obligation.** Someone discovering they need a programme they do not have. The slowest to convert and the most loyal once they do, because you are the one who explained it. This is the same group we described in [legionella](/insights/legionella-the-open-goal) and across [compliance generally](/insights/price-and-regulation-beat-near-me).

None of these are large. All of them are worth a page.

## Price the lifetime, not the invoice

This is the single most common mistake, and it is expensive in both directions.

A firm looks at a contract enquiry, sees a monthly figure, and sets an acquisition budget against it. But a three-year contract at that monthly figure, with a realistic renewal rate and the ad-hoc work that comes alongside it, is worth a multiple of what the first invoice suggests — often five to ten times.

Work out the real figure: monthly value, times realistic contract life, times renewal probability, plus the incidental work the relationship generates. That number is what you can afford to spend to win one. It is almost always far more than firms assume, which is why they under-invest in a channel that would pay back handsomely and conclude it does not work.

## Handle the few enquiries properly

When a quarter's pipeline is a handful of enquiries, enquiry handling stops being an operational detail and becomes the whole strategy.

A missed call on a contract enquiry is not a lost job. It is a lost three years. A buyer testing the market contacts several suppliers in one sitting, and the first substantive response usually shapes the specification everyone else then bids against. [The first five minutes](/insights/the-first-five-minutes) matters more here than anywhere, not less — and the form in front of that enquiry should be [asking questions that qualify](/insights/quotation-forms-that-qualify), because a site visit on the wrong portfolio is a wasted day you cannot spare.
