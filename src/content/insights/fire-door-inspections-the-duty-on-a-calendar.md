Fire door inspection is one of the few parts of the built environment where a regulation recently created a market from almost nothing, and where the firms best placed to serve it have mostly carried on marketing the way they did before.

The Fire Safety (England) Regulations 2022 came into force on 23 January 2023. Among other things, they require the responsible person for a multi-occupied residential building with a storey over 11 metres to check every fire door in the common parts at least every three months, and to use best endeavours to check every flat entrance door at least once a year. Those intervals came straight out of the Grenfell Tower Inquiry's Phase 1 recommendations, and they are not advisory.

Read that again as a marketer rather than as a fire engineer. A statutory duty, on a fixed cycle, applied to a large and knowable stock of buildings, placed on people who in most cases are not competent to discharge it themselves. That is about as good as demand gets.

## Who is actually buying

The instinct is to picture a building owner. The reality is a managing agent with forty blocks, a housing association with a maintenance team that is already stretched, a resident management company whose directors have just been told they are the responsible person, or a facilities manager inheriting a portfolio and an audit date.

Three things follow from that.

**The enquiry is a programme, not a visit.** Nobody with a quarterly duty across a portfolio wants to book inspections one door at a time. They want a supplier who will schedule the cycle, turn up, record the results in a form that will survive an audit, and tell them what needs fixing. The first conversation is about a year of work, and the page that wins it should talk like that.

**The buyer is often new to the duty.** The 2022 Regulations caught a lot of people who had never thought of themselves as responsible persons. The Building Safety Act then widened the net again: since 1 October 2023 every responsible person must record their fire risk assessment in full, which has sent a lot of managing agents back through their obligations line by line. These are people working out what applies to them. That shapes what they type.

**There is usually an incumbent of sorts.** Someone is already doing the fire risk assessment. Someone may be doing a cursory door check as part of it. The inspection firm is often displacing a general fire safety contractor who treats doors as a line item. We have written about what that does to the page in [marketing a recurring contract](/insights/marketing-a-contract-not-a-job), and all of it applies here.

## What they search for

This is where the gap opens up.

Pull the fire safety demand we track and the fire door cluster behaves like every other compliance discipline we run: the obligation and interval questions outnumber the supplier questions, and the supplier questions are the contested ones. People ask whether fire doors need to be inspected, how often, who is allowed to do it, what the inspection involves, what happens if a door fails, and what it costs. They ask these before they ask who to hire, and when they do get to the hiring question, they already have a view of the answer from whoever explained the first part.

The firms competing for this work almost universally answer none of it. Their page is called "Passive Fire Protection" or "Fire Door Services". It lists installation, inspection, maintenance and remediation. It has accreditation logos and a contact form. It does not say that the quarterly duty applies above 11 metres, or that British Standard guidance for non-residential buildings works on a six-month cycle, or that the gap under the door is as important as the strip down the side of it.

So the page cannot rank for the questions, because it does not contain the answers. It can only rank for the supplier phrase, where it competes with every national provider and directory. That is the page-three problem described in [ranking without traffic](/insights/ranking-without-traffic), and in fire doors it is almost total.

## The pages worth building

Not a brochure. A small set of pages, each answering one thing a buyer actually asks, each leading to the same enquiry.

**Do my fire doors have to be inspected?** The obligation page. Which buildings fall under the 2022 Regulations, what the 11-metre line means in practice, what the Fire Safety Order already required before 2023, and what the responsible person is actually liable for. Written for a managing agent who has just discovered the duty, not for a fire engineer.

**How often do fire doors need checking?** The interval page. Quarterly for communal doors above 11 metres. Annually, on a best-endeavours basis, for flat entrance doors. Six-monthly as the standards guidance for other buildings. What "best endeavours" means when a leaseholder will not open the door. This is the page that gets cited, because it is the page that gives a straight answer.

**What does a fire door inspection involve?** The scope page. Gaps, seals, closers, hinges, glazing, signage and the certification mark. What a competent inspector records against each door, and why a tick on a clipboard is not a record. This page sells competence without ever saying the word.

**Who can inspect fire doors?** The competence page. What the Regulations expect of the person doing the check, what third-party certification schemes exist and what they do and do not prove. Honest about the fact that a basic visual check can be done in-house, and clear about where that stops being sensible.

**What does it cost?** The page every firm resists and every buyer wants. A per-door range for a scheduled inspection, what moves it, how it changes across a portfolio, and what a remediation visit typically runs to. [Price and regulation questions beat "near me"](/insights/price-and-regulation-beat-near-me) in every compliance discipline we have looked at. This one is no exception.

Each of those links into one service page for inspection programmes, with a form that asks about the portfolio rather than about the door. Number of blocks, storeys, approximate door count, whether there is an existing register, when the next audit is due. The answers decide whether this is a site visit or a phone call, and that is the whole point of asking.

## What the enquiry is worth

Work this out before deciding what you can afford to spend to win one.

Take the quarterly inspection fee across a block. Multiply by four, and then by the realistic life of the relationship, which for a managing agent who is happy is years rather than months. Add the flat entrance door programme. Then add the part that most firms forget to price in: the remediation that an honest inspection generates. Closers that no longer close. Seals that have been painted over. Doors with a 10-millimetre gap at the threshold. A proportion of every inspection round becomes repair, adjustment or replacement, and the firm that found the fault is the firm that gets asked to fix it.

Then there is the wider survey. A client who trusts you with the doors is the obvious client to ask about compartmentation, fire stopping and the rest of the passive fire picture. The inspection is the cheapest way into that conversation that exists.

Set that figure against the cost of the enquiry and the maths looks very different from a per-door price list. Most firms we speak to have never done the calculation. The ones who have tend to stop describing inspection as low-margin work.

## Separate the buyers

One last point, because it decides how many pages this takes. A managing agent with a quarterly duty across residential blocks and a main contractor who needs doors certified on a new build are not the same buyer. They search differently, buy differently, and are triggered by different events. One wants a programme and a record; the other wants a certificate and a date. Both can be served by the same firm. They should not be served by the same page. The test for when a service earns a second page is set out in [how many pages a multi-trade firm actually needs](/insights/how-many-pages-does-a-multi-trade-firm-need), and fire doors pass it comfortably.

## The short version

A regulation wrote the demand onto a calendar. The buyers are working out their obligations in public, by typing them into a search box. The firms who could answer them are, by and large, describing their services instead.

If you run a passive fire business or a [fire protection firm](/fire-protection) that does doors as part of the mix, this is the discipline where the gap between what is asked and what is answered is widest, and it is the one where a handful of honest pages would put you in front of the right buyer first. The same shape holds across the [compliance disciplines](/compliance) we work in. Fire doors are simply the clearest case of it.
